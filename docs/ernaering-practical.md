# Applied nutrition question evidence

Internal maintenance notes, checked 1 November 2026. These notes are not student-facing sources or new curriculum requirements.

## Trans fat

- User-supplied background: [Bramat, Derfor er transfett farlig](https://bramat.no/kosthold/naeringsstoffer/101-derfor-er-transfett-farlig). Its main health and partial-hardening explanations support the tasks. Do not generalize its product examples into a claim that current Norwegian biscuits, cakes or fries invariably contain substantial trans fat.
- [WHO, Trans fat](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat), 24 January 2024: trans fatty acids are chemically unsaturated, with industrial partial hydrogenation a major source. High-temperature frying produces modest increases compared with partially hydrogenated oils. Ordinary cis-unsaturated fat is a preferable replacement.
- [Helsedirektoratet, Blir umettet fett omdannet til transfett ved steking?](https://www.helsedirektoratet.no/rapporter/ekspertuttalelse-om-fett/blir-umettet-fett-omdannet-til-transfett-ved-steking), last updated 17 February 2021: ordinary frying/baking does not automatically create trans fat; long, repeated frying can increase it. Heat also causes oxidation and other deterioration. Old frying oil is therefore not a reliable trans-fat identification cue on its own.
- [Mattilsynet, Veileder om ernæringspåstander](https://www.mattilsynet.no/mat-og-drikke/merking-av-mat/ernaerings-og-helsepastander/veileder-om-ernaeringspastander): industrial trans fat is limited to 2 g per 100 g fat. This is provenance for the current-product qualification, not a value pupils must memorize.

`practical-fat-health` uses three options because cis and unsaturated are overlapping categories; trans fat is also chemically unsaturated. `practical-trans-fat-foods` uses a historical ingredient-list clue and qualifies the frying association. It does not claim every heated oil or modern industrial bakery product is high in trans fat.

## Cooked versus raw egg protein

- [Evenepoel et al. (1998), Digestibility of cooked and raw egg protein in humans as assessed by stable isotope techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9772141/), *Journal of Nutrition* 128(10):1716–1722, DOI [10.1093/jn/128.10.1716](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/128.10.1716): five ileostomy patients, crossover test with 25 g labelled egg protein. True ileal digestibility averaged 90.9% cooked versus 51.3% raw. These small-study values are not presented as universal percentages for one egg.
- [Evenepoel et al. (1999), Amount and fate of egg protein escaping assimilation in the small intestine of humans](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10564098/): ten healthy volunteers; substantially less cooked than raw egg protein escaped small-intestinal digestion and absorption.

`practical-egg-protein` asks about digestion and absorption, not protein created by cooking or guaranteed training gains. Denaturation changes protein structure and accessibility. Exact experimental percentages are not required knowledge.

## Meal illustration and model data

`practical-meals` is an original, brand-free illustrative comparison: A depicts a small burger and fries; B depicts a larger plate with rice, chicken and vegetables plus fruit. The task explicitly supplies constructed example data, A = 300 g / 600 kcal and B = 600 g / 600 kcal. These numbers are teaching data, not measured nutritional claims about the drawn meals or reproductions of values in the user-supplied memes.

`practical-meal-energy-density` assesses 2 versus 1 kcal per gram. `practical-meal-nutrients` assesses the limits of inference: equal energy does not prove equal nutrient composition, and the artwork alone cannot establish precise vitamin/mineral quantities. Neither task labels foods morally, claims one meal determines diet quality, guarantees satiety, or recommends a body-weight target.
